### Life Under the Ice (`a01-lake-ice`, pages 1–2; 301 words FK 6.53)

On a cold January morning, a lake in Minnesota can look completely still. A thick lid of ice covers the surface. Yet beneath that frozen lid, the lake is full of life. Fish swim slowly through the dark water, and turtles rest in the mud at the bottom. They survive because of an unusual property of water and because each animal has its own way of coping with the cold.

Most substances shrink and grow denser as they cool. Water behaves differently. As it cools, it becomes denser only until it reaches about 39 degrees Fahrenheit. Below that temperature, it begins to expand again, and when it freezes, it becomes lighter still, which is why ice floats. As a result, the coldest water and the ice stay at the top, while slightly warmer, heavier water sinks to the bottom. A frozen lake is really a layered lake.

The ice also works like a blanket. It keeps the warmer water below from losing its heat to the freezing air. If ice sank instead of floating, many lakes would freeze from the bottom up.

Within this protected space, animals have developed remarkable strategies. Many fish slow their bodies down. Their hearts beat more slowly, and they need far less food and oxygen than they do in summer. Painted turtles go even further. A turtle can stay buried in the mud for months without coming up to breathe, absorbing a little oxygen through its skin.

Winter under the ice is not easy, though. When deep snow piles on top of the ice, it blocks sunlight, and underwater plants stop producing oxygen. In a long winter, the oxygen in a shallow lake can run low. Even so, the lake's layers and its animals' patient habits usually carry them through until the ice melts in spring.

### The Clever Octopus (`a02-octopus`, pages 3–4; 312 words FK 7.83)

A diver swimming over a rocky reef may pass right by an octopus without ever noticing it. One moment the animal looks like a patch of brown seaweed, and the next it seems to vanish against the stones. The octopus is famous for two talents. It is one of the best hiders in the ocean, and it is also one of the cleverest problem-solvers among animals without a backbone.

The octopus hides by changing the color of its skin. Its skin contains thousands of tiny cells called chromatophores, and each one holds a small sac of pigment, such as yellow, red, or brown. Muscles around each sac can stretch it open or squeeze it shut. When a sac opens, its color spreads across a wider spot of skin. Because these muscles are controlled directly by nerves, an octopus can change its whole pattern in less than a second.

Color is only part of the disguise. An octopus can also raise small bumps on its skin to copy the rough surface of a rock or the frilly edges of a plant. By combining color, pattern, and texture, it can blend into many different backgrounds.

The same quick nervous system helps explain the animal's intelligence. An octopus has hundreds of millions of nerve cells, and a large share of them are found in its eight arms rather than in its brain. Each arm can explore, touch, and grip on its own while the brain directs the overall plan.

Scientists have watched octopuses solve problems in surprising ways. In aquariums, some have learned to twist open jars to reach a crab inside. In the wild, certain octopuses collect empty coconut shells and carry them along, later fitting the two halves together as a portable shelter. Their ability to hide and their ability to think both help them survive in an ocean full of hungry predators.

### Arches and Cables (`a03-arch-bridges`, pages 5–6; 316 words FK 6.74)

Every bridge has the same basic job. It must hold up its own weight along with everything that crosses it. Engineers have found different shapes that do this job well, and two of the most famous are the arch and the suspension bridge. Yet before any large bridge is built, engineers also make sure their design will work by testing it first.

An arch bridge carries weight by pushing. When a load presses down on top of a curved arch, the force travels along the curve toward both ends. The stones or steel in the arch are squeezed together, and the ends push outward and downward into strong supports on the banks. Stone is very good at being squeezed, which is why some stone arch bridges built by the ancient Romans are still standing today.

A suspension bridge works in almost the opposite way. Its road hangs from long vertical cables, and those cables hang from two huge main cables draped over tall towers. Instead of being squeezed, the cables are pulled tight. The main cables carry the load to the towers and to heavy anchors buried at each end. This design lets suspension bridges stretch across very wide spaces.

Because a full-size bridge is so costly and so important, engineers do not simply build one and hope for the best. They first test their ideas on models. Much of this testing happens on computers, which calculate how each part will respond to heavy traffic.

Engineers also build small physical models. For long suspension bridges, they often place a scale model in a wind tunnel and blow air past it to see whether the deck will twist or sway. If the model moves too much, they can change the shape of the deck before construction begins. In this way, the careful study of forces and the careful testing of models work together to keep bridges strong.

### The Waggle Dance (`a06-honeybee-dance`, pages 11–12; 293 words FK 7.15)

Inside a dark beehive, a worker bee returns from a field of flowers and begins to dance. She runs forward in a straight line while shaking her body from side to side, then circles back and repeats the move. The other bees crowd around her and pay close attention. The dance was explained in detail by the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch, who studied bees for many years. He found that the straight part of the dance, called the waggle run, points the way to food. The angle of the run on the upright honeycomb matches the angle between the food and the sun. The length of the waggle run tells how far away the food is, since a longer run means a longer trip.

The watching bees use this information to fly straight to the flowers. In this way, a single scout can send many workers to a rich source of nectar and pollen.

As bees visit those flowers, they do more than gather food. Pollen sticks to their fuzzy bodies, and some of it rubs off on the next flower they visit. This transfer of pollen allows many plants to produce fruit and seeds.

Farmers depend on this work. Crops such as apples, cherries, blueberries, and almonds all need pollinators to produce a good harvest. In California, beekeepers bring hives to almond orchards each spring so the bees can pollinate the blossoms. Other insects and animals pollinate plants too, but honeybees are especially useful because they live in large colonies that can be moved. Every waggle dance helps send those busy pollinators to work.

### Islands from Fire (`a07-volcanic-islands`, pages 13–14; 306 words FK 6.81)

The islands of Hawaii rise from the middle of the Pacific Ocean, thousands of miles from any continent. They were not carried there. Instead, they were built from the seafloor up, one layer of hardened lava at a time. The same volcanic rock that built these islands has also given them rich, fertile farmland.

Deep beneath Hawaii lies a hot spot, an area where melted rock rises from far inside the Earth. When this hot rock breaks through the ocean floor, it pours out as lava and cools into new stone. Over a very long time, eruption after eruption piles up layers until a mountain reaches the surface of the sea and becomes an island.

The hot spot stays in roughly the same place, but the Pacific Plate, the huge slab of rock that carries the seafloor, slowly moves a few inches each year. As the plate drifts, older islands are carried away from the hot spot and new ones form behind them. This is why the islands to the northwest, such as Kauai, are older than the Big Island of Hawaii.

Once the lava cools, a slower process begins. Rain, wind, and plant roots gradually break the dark rock into smaller pieces. Over many years, this weathered rock becomes soil that is full of minerals plants need. Volcanic soil also tends to hold water well, which helps crops grow.

Farmers in many parts of the world take advantage of this soil. On the slopes of Hawaii's Big Island, growers raise coffee in fields of volcanic soil. In Italy, orchards and vineyards cover the lower slopes around Mount Etna. Rich soil like this takes a long time to form, so the oldest islands often have the deepest soil. In this way, the slow work of volcanoes builds both land and the farms that grow on it.

### Letters Made of Metal (`a08-printing-press`, pages 15–16; 302 words FK 8.35)

For centuries, most books in Europe were copied by hand. A single book could take a skilled scribe months to finish, so books were rare and costly. Around 1450, a German metalworker named Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing system that changed this. His invention depended on a clever method called movable type, and it helped spread reading and ideas across Europe.

Movable type means that every letter is a separate small piece. Gutenberg cast each letter in metal using a hand mold, so he could quickly make many identical copies of the same letter. A printer arranged the letters one by one into words and lines, then locked the lines tightly into a frame to form a whole page. After the page was printed, the letters could be taken apart and reused.

Gutenberg also needed the right ink and the right press. He used an oily ink that clung to metal type better than the watery inks used for writing. His press, which worked somewhat like the presses used to squeeze grapes, pushed paper firmly and evenly against the inked letters. Printing with movable type had been invented earlier in China and Korea, but Gutenberg's system suited the small number of letters in European alphabets especially well.

The results spread quickly. Within a few decades, print shops had opened in many cities across Europe, and millions of books had been printed by the year 1500. Because printed books cost far less than handwritten ones, more families, schools, and libraries could afford them.

As books became common, more people had reasons to learn to read. Printing also made ideas travel. A scientist could publish a discovery, and readers in distant cities could study the exact same words and pictures. Gutenberg's metal letters helped turn reading from a rare skill into an everyday one.

### Long Flights Home (`a09-bird-migration`, pages 17–18; 294 words FK 7.02)

### The Tiny Water Bear (`a10-tardigrades`, pages 19–20; 314 words FK 8.32)

### Life in the Dry Desert (`a11-desert-life`, pages 21–22; 326 words FK 7.13)

In a hot desert, months can pass without a drop of rain, and summer afternoons can be fiercely hot. Yet deserts are full of living things that have found their own ways to handle these harsh conditions. Desert plants are experts at storing and saving water, while many desert animals survive by simply staying out of the heat.

Cactuses show how well a plant can store water. A cactus has a thick, fleshy stem filled with spongy tissue that soaks up water after a rainstorm. The giant saguaro of the Sonoran Desert has folds along its stem, like the pleats of an accordion. When it rains, the folds spread apart so the stem can swell and hold more water.

Cactuses are just as good at saving the water they collect. Instead of broad leaves, which would lose water to the dry air, most cactuses have sharp spines. A waxy coating on the stem helps seal water inside. Many cactuses also open the tiny pores in their skin only at night, when the air is cooler, so less water escapes. Their roots spread wide and shallow, ready to catch rain before it sinks away.

Desert animals rely more on timing and shelter. Many of them are nocturnal, which means they rest during the day and come out at night.
